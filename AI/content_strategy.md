# Content Strategy

## Target Audience
1. **Primary:** Hiring managers at mobile gaming studios looking for PO/PM roles
2. **Secondary:** Recruiters scanning portfolios for shortlisting
3. **Tertiary:** Peers and collaborators evaluating credibility

## Voice & Tone

### What the portfolio already does well (preserve this):
- Direct, non-buzzword language
- Problem-first framing ("Casual games often have a mid-week engagement gap")
- Honest self-assessment ("I don't call myself a Product Owner by title")
- Concrete actions over vague claims ("Defined the core feature loop, event cadence, and risk-reward logic")
- Human, conversational tone that still feels professional

### Voice rules:
- **DO:** "Turn [broad concept] into [concrete deliverable]"
- **DO:** "Support [process] through [specific mechanism]"
- **DO:** "I [verb] because [honest reason]"
- **DON'T:** "Leveraged," "drove growth," "passionate about," "synergy," "innovative"
- **DON'T:** Empty claims without evidence ("dramatically improved engagement")
- **DON'T:** Passive voice ("the system was designed to..." → "I designed the system to...")

### Sentence/paragraph length:
- Sentences: 15-25 words ideal
- Paragraphs: 2-3 sentences max in case studies
- Lists over paragraphs when enumerating capabilities
- List items start with action verbs or arrows (→)

## Case Study Structure Standard

Use the **Lucky Tower / SuperPlay pattern** (the stronger structure):

1. **Hero** — Project title + one-line subtitle explaining what this is
2. **Meta grid** — Role, Timeline, Platform, Focus
3. **Screens/Gallery** — Visual evidence (screenshots, videos, GIFs)
4. **Product Goal** — What this should achieve, in one paragraph
5. **My Role** — 3-5 bullet points of what I specifically did
6. **Problem / Opportunity** — Why this project exists
7. **What I Defined** — Concrete deliverables and decisions
8. **How I Worked with Engineering / QA** — Collaboration and handoff
9. **Metrics or Success Criteria** — How success is measured
10. **Iteration / What Changed** — What I learned and adjusted
11. **Next Iterations** — What comes next (shows forward thinking)
12. **CTA** — Link to live demo or back to portfolio

### Older pattern to avoid (ICE Analytics style):
- Overview → Role → Problem → Solution → What It Includes → Impact → What I Learned
- This is flatter, less structured, and doesn't show product thinking as clearly

## Project Descriptions (for cards)

Each card description should:
- Start with an action verb (Designed, Built, Shipped, Created)
- Name the specific deliverable, not the general category
- Include at least one concrete detail (KPI plan, level editor, snake draft)
- Be 1-2 sentences, under 40 words
- End with "why it matters" not "what the tech is"

**Good example (Lucky Tower):**
"Designed a weekly risk-reward event with reward logic, KPI planning, and tuning hooks to test a stronger mid-week engagement loop."

**Bad example (generic):**
"Created a game feature to improve player engagement using modern game design principles."

## Writing for Recruiters vs Developers

Recruiters care about:
- What role did you play?
- What was the outcome?
- Is this person senior enough / relevant enough?
- Can I quickly explain this candidate to the hiring manager?

Developers/hiring managers care about:
- How did you think about this problem?
- What tradeoffs did you make?
- Do you understand the systems behind the feature?
- Can you work with engineering and QA?

The portfolio should serve both: **cards and headlines for recruiters, detailed sections for hiring managers.**

## Handling Solo Work Honestly

Most projects are solo prototypes. Frame them as:
- "I built this to test [hypothesis]" — shows product thinking
- "I prototyped this to validate [concept] before larger investment" — shows MVP discipline
- Avoid implying team leadership when there was no team
- The SuperPlay case study is the exception — emphasize the cross-team coordination there
