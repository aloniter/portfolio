BlockQuest

A Unity puzzle-battler where clearing a line doesn't score points. It takes a chunk out of the boss standing over your board.

Role
Solo — design and build
Platform
Unity · portrait mobile
Status
Built, release readiness in progress
Instrumented
Analytics, Remote Config, crash reporting

The game is built and instrumented; getting it release-ready is what's left. It isn't on the App Store yet, and I'm not going to write it up as though it is.

Clear a line, the boss takes the hit

Problem

Block puzzles are easy to pick up and easy to put down. Every clear feels like the last one, so after ten minutes there's no reason to keep going. I kept coming back to one idea: what if the thing you're clearing lines against could hit you back? Everyone already knows how block puzzles work, and everyone already knows how boss fights work. I wanted to see what happens when one drives the other.

The hard part isn't either half. Puzzle rules are fine on their own and boss rules are fine on their own — putting them on the same board is where it gets ugly. If a boss attack doesn't really wreck your plan, the fight is decoration. If it wrecks it too hard, the board turns to mush and you can't read it. The whole job is building pressure that still feels fair while it's actively ruining your day, on a phone screen, with nowhere to put a tutorial.

The game

Decisions

Boss attacks hit the board, not a timer

The easy version is a countdown: beat the boss before time runs out. I made attacks drop obstacles onto the board instead, so the fight and the puzzle are the same problem rather than two things happening near each other. What it costs — much harder to keep fair. One badly timed attack reads as bad luck, not difficulty. Every attack is telegraphed a turn early so you can see it coming and play around it.

The board stays at 8×8

A bigger grid opens up better combos. I stayed at 8×8 because the board and the boss's health bar have to fit in one glance on a phone held upright. What it costs — no room for elaborate multi-line setups. Worth it: the boss layer already asks a lot of a new player, and the grid shouldn't add to that.

Three pieces in the tray instead of one

One random piece per turn is far easier to balance. Three means planning several moves ahead and choosing an order, which is where the good decisions live. What it costs — more to think about every turn, so what shows up in the tray and when it refreshes took real tuning. A mobile session should feel quick, not exhausting.

Instrument it before launch, not after

Analytics, crash reporting and Remote Config went in as part of building the game rather than as a pre-launch scramble, because a boss HP curve tuned on my own taste is worth very little once real players touch it. What it costs — real time spent on plumbing that adds nothing to how the game feels today. It buys the ability to change the difficulty after launch without shipping a build.

Build

  • Game design: the loop end to end — placement, clears, boss damage, counter-attacks, progression
  • Unity implementation: the 8×8 board, three-piece tray, HP states, and a UI that survives a phone screen
  • FTUE: teaching the board, the tray and the boss objective in the first few seconds, without a tutorial screen
  • Instrumentation: Firebase Analytics and GameAnalytics for the funnel, Crashlytics for stability, Remote Config so balance can move without a release
  • Feel: what a combo feels like, what taking a hit feels like, what losing feels like
  • Scope: every call on what made the cut, with no design or engineering team to hand anything to

Where it stands

  • Built Full loop, FTUE and progression working end to end
  • Instrumented Analytics, crash reporting and Remote Config live and validated
  • 1 turn Warning before every boss attack lands

It isn't public yet, and that's the honest limit of this one. What it does show is that I can take a genuinely awkward design problem — two mechanics fighting over one board — and carry it solo from Unity architecture through UI and FTUE to a build that's set up to be tuned on real numbers instead of my own opinion.

What's left: release readiness, and then the part I actually want, which is finding out where real players quit and moving the boss HP curve to meet them.
