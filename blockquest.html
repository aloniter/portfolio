Pre-launch

BlockQuest

A puzzle battler where every line you clear hits the boss standing over your board.

Designed, built and instrumented end to end.

  • Unity
  • C#
  • Firebase
  • GameAnalytics

01

Can a familiar block-puzzle loop feel like a fight?

Block puzzles are easy to pick up and easy to put down. Every clear feels like the last one, so after ten minutes there is no reason to make the next one.

Everyone already knows how block puzzles work. Everyone already knows how boss fights work. The whole project is one bet: that putting them on the same board makes both of them better.

The hard part is neither half. Puzzle rules are fine on their own, boss rules are fine on their own, and putting them on one board is where it gets ugly. If an attack does not really wreck your plan, the fight is decoration. If it wrecks it too hard, the board turns to mush and you cannot read it. The job is building pressure that still feels fair while it is actively ruining your day.

02

Clearing a line doesn't score points.

It takes a chunk out of the boss. Score is the number a block puzzle gives you when it has nothing else to offer; here the same action moves the only bar that decides whether you win.

03

The boss had to change the puzzle, not sit above it.

The easy version of this game is a countdown: beat the boss before the timer runs out. Attacks drop blocks onto the board instead, so the fight and the puzzle are one problem rather than two things happening near each other.

Board space is the real currency in a block puzzle. Making that the thing the boss spends is what turns an opponent into a mechanic.

What it costs — far harder to keep fair. One badly timed attack reads as bad luck rather than difficulty, and a player who feels cheated does not open the app again.

04

You see the attack before you have to solve it.

Every attack is announced ahead of time: which ability is coming, and how many turns until it lands. Difficulty becomes something you read and plan around instead of something that happens to you.

It also gives a loss an address. You can point at the turn where you spent the wrong space, which is the difference between a game that feels hard and a game that feels rigged.

05

The first fight is the tutorial.

There is nowhere to put a tutorial on a phone screen that someone opened to play a block puzzle. So the opening encounter does the teaching: a boss slow enough and soft enough to let the loop explain itself — place, clear, watch the bar move — before anything starts pushing back.

What it costs — the first fight is not allowed to be interesting on its own terms. It is a teaching instrument, and it gets tuned as one.

06

Three bosses a world, each asking the board for something different.

Variety here is not new art on the same fight. Each boss spends board space differently, so the thing you are planning around changes while the rules you learned in the first fight stay true.

Campaign is the taught, tuned path through them. Last Stand sits behind it — the campaign ends, and the reason to keep playing shouldn't.

07

A boss curve tuned on my own taste is worth very little.

I have played this game more than anyone alive, which makes me the worst possible judge of how hard the third fight is. Analytics, crash reporting and remote configuration went in while the game was being built rather than as a pre-launch scramble.

The capability that matters: boss balance moves without shipping a build. If a fight turns out to be where people stop, the numbers change and the next encounter uses them — no release, no review queue, no waiting a week to learn whether the change helped.

What it costs — real time on plumbing that adds nothing to how the game feels today. It buys every decision made after launch.

08

The decision I'd defend

Building the whole thing changed how I think about difficulty. The boss HP curve was the easy part — it is a value in a config file, and it can move remotely on a Tuesday afternoon.

Whether a loss feels earned is not a number, and no amount of tuning fixes it afterwards. That is what the telegraph is for, and it is the least visible thing in the game: four small orbs under a health bar. It is also the one decision the whole idea rests on, because unfair is the one thing a puzzle player will not forgive.

The loop, the FTUE, the progression and the instrumentation are done. Release readiness is what is left, and it isn't on the App Store yet.
