The Evening Job Nobody Wanted

An inspector walks a building and comes back with a hundred photos and a page of notes. Then the real work starts: sitting down that evening, matching every photo to what was wrong with it, and typing it all into a document. Hours per job, every job, and the longer the gap between seeing the problem and writing it down, the easier it is to miss something.

A Data Problem Wearing a UI

This looks like an app-design job and isn't. It's a data problem in a trench coat. Photos, annotations, and findings all have to stay tied together while someone moves fast through a site with a phone in one hand, and the numbered list at the end has to come out right, every time, without anyone retyping it. Get that structure wrong and the export quietly produces a broken report. And this one goes to actual paying clients, so "looks fine in a demo" was never the bar.

How It Works on a Job

1. Open the job

Site, date, notes. Everything from that inspection lives in one place instead of scattered across a camera roll.

2. Shoot and mark

Photograph the problem and circle it right there, while you're standing in front of it and still remember what it was.

3. The list writes itself

Every marked photo becomes a numbered finding. Nothing to retype, so nothing quietly falls off the list.

4. Export and send

Pick PDF or Word and you get the finished document: photos, numbered findings, ready for the client.

Three Decisions That Shaped It

Photos first, forms never

Inspectors work with a camera, not a keyboard. So the app opens on the camera, not on a form waiting to be filled in. What it costs: all the complexity moves into annotating and organising afterwards, and that part has to be quick enough that nobody's standing in a stairwell waiting for it.

The findings list builds itself

Mark a photo and it becomes a numbered finding automatically. No separate step where you write the list again. That's the whole reason things stopped getting missed. What it costs: the data model had to be right on day one, because the entire report is generated from it. Get it wrong later and you're rebuilding the foundation.

Exporting to Word and PDF, boring as that is

I could have built a much nicer report viewer inside the app. Nobody wanted one. They wanted the file they already send to clients, in the formats those clients already expect. Word when someone needs to edit it, PDF when it's final. What it costs: two real export pipelines to build and keep working, instead of a pretty screen. That's the line between something people demo and something people use.

What the Job Taught Me

  • Taking the photos was never the problem: turning them into a document was
  • Fast beats pretty: nobody cares how the annotation screen looks if it lags
  • The report has to come out identical every time: same numbering, same fields, no surprises
  • Next up: templates per client, accounts for individual inspectors, a shared company workspace, and better-looking exports (cover page, signature, logo)
