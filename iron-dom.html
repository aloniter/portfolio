Iron Dom

An arcade-defense game with one mechanic and three numbers. You understand it in two seconds and you're still losing to it a minute later.

Play Iron Dom

The game is in Hebrew, since it was built for friends here, and the cities you defend are real ones. Everything below is described in English.

Tap the sky, launch an interceptor

Problem

I wanted to see how much tension you could get out of almost no rules. One mechanic: intercept the thing before it lands. Everything else comes from pacing: no upgrade trees, no currencies, nothing to read before you start.

Tense is easy. Tense and fair is the actual work. Throw more at the player until they lose and you have tension. Every second also has to stay readable: you see the threat, you react, and you know straight away whether that worked. The moment the screen stops being readable, losing feels like the game cheated.

Decisions

Tap anywhere, don't aim

I could have made you drag a crosshair and line up a shot. Instead you tap where you want the interceptor to go and it goes. What it costs: a skill ceiling. There's no aiming technique to master, so expert play looks a lot like decent play. Worth it: the game had to work one-handed on a phone, and nobody's dragging a reticle while a wave is coming in.

Twenty interceptors, not unlimited

With infinite ammo the correct strategy is to tap constantly and the game stops being about judgement. Capping the round makes every launch a decision. What it costs: you can lose by running dry, which feels harsh the first time. It's also the constraint that turns tapping into choosing.

Five hits, not one

One hit and out would be more dramatic and more honest to the theme. I gave the city five, because a new player needs to be allowed to misread the first wave and still be playing thirty seconds later. What it costs: a little tension per hit, bought back by people actually getting far enough to learn the pacing.

Real cities, real skylines

The backgrounds are recognisable: you can pick out the towers in Tel Aviv. It makes the thing you're protecting feel specific instead of generic. What it costs: it's very local, and it won't read the same way to someone who's never been here. I kept it anyway, because the specificity is the point.

Result

  • 20 Interceptors a round: every launch is a choice
  • 5 Hits before the city falls
  • 3 Cities, each with its own skyline

Those three numbers are the whole game. Everything else, the pacing, the readability, and the way a wave builds, exists to make those constraints feel fair rather than mean.

Every time I added a system it got worse. The version that worked came from taking things out: cleaner threats, faster feedback on a hit, and difficulty that climbs while the goal stays completely obvious. Restraint did more for this one than any feature.

Stack

  • JavaScript
  • Mobile + browser
  • Wave pacing
  • Solo build

If I picked it back up: a few threat types that change what you go for first without adding rules, combo bonuses so a good streak pays off, and tracking survival and hit rate so I could tune off numbers instead of feel.

Go lose to it.

I lost my first round in about fifteen seconds, and I made it.
