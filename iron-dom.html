Iron Dom

A complete arcade-defense game with reaction-based interception and escalating wave pressure, designed and built solo from concept to a fully playable build.

Role Solo Designer & Developer
Timeline Personal project
Platform Mobile / Browser
Focus Reaction controls and wave pressure

The game is in Hebrew, since it was built for friends here, and the cities you defend are real ones. Everything below is described in English.

Outcome

A finished, playable game: three cities, twenty interceptors, five hits before you lose it. Built solo and still live, and it's genuinely hard: I lost my first round in about fifteen seconds.

It In Motion

How Few Rules Can You Get Away With?

I wanted to see how much tension you could get out of almost no rules. One mechanic: intercept the thing before it lands. Everything else comes from pacing. No upgrade trees, no currencies, nothing to read before you start.

The Whole Thing

  • Designed the whole defense loop, from idea to something you can actually play
  • Built the game flow, input handling, collision logic, and scoring
  • Tuned the waves so they get harder without the screen turning to noise
  • Made sure every threat, action, and result reads instantly

Tense Is Easy. Fair Is Not.

Making something tense is easy. Throw more at the player until they lose. Making it tense and fair is the actual work. Every second has to stay readable: you see the threat, you react, and you know straight away whether that worked. The moment the screen stops being readable, losing feels like the game cheated.

The Entire Game

  • Three cities to defend: Tel Aviv, Jerusalem, Haifa, each with its own skyline behind you
  • Tap anywhere on the sky to launch an interceptor. That's the entire control scheme
  • Twenty interceptors per round, so you can't just spam the screen and hope
  • Five hits before the city falls and the round ends
  • A permanent readout of score, hits taken, and interceptors spent, so you always know where you stand

Four Constraints I Picked on Purpose

Tap anywhere, don't aim

I could have made you drag a crosshair and line up a shot. Instead you tap where you want the interceptor to go and it goes. What it costs: a skill ceiling. There's no aiming technique to master, so expert play looks a lot like decent play. Worth it, because the game had to work one-handed on a phone, and nobody's dragging a reticle while a wave is coming in.

Twenty interceptors, not unlimited

With infinite ammo the correct strategy is to tap constantly, and the game stops being about judgement. Capping the round at twenty makes every launch a decision. What it costs: you can lose by running dry, which feels harsh the first time it happens, but it's the constraint that turns tapping into choosing.

Five hits, not one

One hit and out would be more dramatic and more honest to the theme. I gave the city five, because a new player needs to be allowed to misread the first wave and still be playing thirty seconds later. What it costs: a little tension per hit, bought back by people actually getting far enough to learn the pacing.

Real cities, real skylines

The backgrounds are recognisable. You can pick out the towers in Tel Aviv. It makes the thing you're protecting feel specific instead of generic. What it costs: it's very local, and it won't read the same way to someone who's never been here. I kept it anyway, because the specificity is the point.

Three Numbers

20 Interceptors a round, every launch is a choice
5 Hits before the city falls
3 Cities, each with its own skyline

Those three numbers are the whole game. Everything else (the pacing, the readability, the way a wave builds) exists to make those constraints feel fair rather than mean. It's live, it's finished, and it does the one thing I wanted: you understand it in about two seconds and you're still losing to it a minute later.

What I Took Out

Every time I added a system it got worse. The version that actually worked came from taking things out: cleaner threats, faster feedback on a hit, and difficulty that climbs while the goal stays completely obvious. Restraint did more for this one than any feature.

If I Picked It Back Up

  • A few threat types that change what you go for first, without adding rules
  • Combo or accuracy bonuses, so a good streak actually pays off
  • Track survival, hit rate, and misses so I can tune off numbers
  • Better start, pause, and restart on mobile
